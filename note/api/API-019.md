The checked kernels are the default. Use an `_unchecked` kernel only where the caller checks the
state itself, or need not stop at the first non-finite component, and the kernel is a measured
cost: the saving is a fraction of a nanosecond per component.

- Why it is faster: one pass with no branch, which the compilers vectorize; the checked kernels
  make two passes and can return at the first non-finite component (KERNEL-002).
- Measured by `script/benchmark.sh cc gcc-15` on 2026-10-18 on an Apple M4 Mac (Apple clang
  21.0.0, Homebrew GCC 15.3.0, Release `-O3`, `d` = 64, 1024 and 16384, in-cache data), in ns per
  component, checked versus unchecked:
  - Euler step 0.44 to 0.53 versus 0.10 to 0.18 (ratio 2.9 to 4.8); RK4 stage 0.45 to 0.58
    versus 0.10 to 0.20 (2.9 to 4.8); RK4 step 0.91 to 1.28 versus 0.25 to 0.42 (2.2 to 4.6).
  - With the right-hand side `dy[i] = -0.5 * y[i]`: a whole Euler step is 1.6 to 3.3 times
    faster; a whole RK4 step takes 2.8 to 3.8 versus 0.8 to 2.0, a saving of 1.4 to 2.0.
- So the gain matters only when the right-hand side costs well under a few nanoseconds per
  component. The figures depend on machine and compiler; elsewhere treat them as orders of
  magnitude, and re-run the benchmark before relying on them.
- Checking afterwards is exact: a finiteness test of `y_out` after the call rejects exactly what
  the checked kernel would (API-018).
